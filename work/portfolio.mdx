When I first thought about making a new portfolio, I was a bit overwhelmed. Now that anyone can quickly spin out a nice-looking landing page, I needed to show that my skills as a designer and engineer aren't something that can be replicated in minutes by an agent. I think that humans are still the ones with vision, the experience to break down implementation steps effectively, the judgment to use AI tools strategically, and the nuance to let the story behind a project shine through. These are the things that will set a design or site apart and I wanted to show that with my portfolio.

The site is built in layers. At first glance it's a clean, simple landing page: a pond, five lily pads, my career as a path of lotuses. That's enough for someone who wants a quick look. But there's a lot more underneath for anyone who's interested: full case studies, essays, a skills reference, a changelog of my career, an API about me, and an assistant that can answer questions.

That second layer is where Mintlify comes in. Good docs tell a story about a product, much like a good portfolio tells a story about a person, and Mintlify is built for exactly that kind of depth: navigation, search, an AI assistant, and pages that agents can read. I use it as the framework for everything behind the landing page, and put my own design on the surface.

## Familiarity and novelty

The redesign started from one goal: build a delightful interface that balances two things.

  <Card title="Novelty" icon="sparkles">

The homepage is the clearest example. Each lily pad is a familiar nav link, but hovering one opens a small surprise: Skills fans out a ring of icons, About Me fans out photos, Writing and Case Studies fan out screenshots. The sidebar, search and "Ask about me" button work the way you'd expect, styled to belong in the pond.

## Building my design sense

I wanted this project to push my UI skills. I already have the familiarity with interfaces and the engineering to build them; what I need to grow is my design sense, the eye for what makes an interface delightful rather than just functional.

How I'm working on it:

- **Look at other people's work.** See what other designers and design engineers are making, find what inspires me about it and what I can learn from it, then refine my own work.
- **Seek out senior designers.** Talk to them, and go to design events. I want exposure to people in the field who can help answer the question: how does the younger generation fill this gap and hone its design sense?
- **Keep a notebook of inspiration.** One moment of delight a day, from an interface or anywhere else.

## The lab

The biggest change to how I worked was building a [design lab](/lab) alongside the site. My favorite Figma habit was duplicating a frame and changing one thing, then comparing rows of versions side by side. Most AI coding workflows lose that: you see one output, it looks good enough, and there's little reason to question it.

In the lab, the coding agent builds several versions of a screen as separate frames, so I can compare them and make an actual decision. I used it to test:

- **Layouts:** six arrangements of the homepage's first screen, and ways to show each career stop along the river.
- **Art direction:** soft 3D vs. watercolor, several painted pond backgrounds, softer takes on the lily pads.
- **Fonts and colors:** a font picker across every option, and a tinted color scale pulled from the painting so the inner pages match the homepage.
- **Whole new directions:** a blueprint, a night sky, a trail map, a code editor and a magazine index, to make sure the pond was a choice and not a default.

A process log records what I tried, kept and dropped each round, and a design system page documents what shipped.

## Process

### Version 1: the editorial site

I made the first version quickly with AI tools, mostly Claude. I started the scaffolds with mock data only, and filled in my own content afterwards.

I tried a few visual directions (magazine, brutalist, notebook) and chose a neutral magazine theme. The API-style skills reference and the career changelog came out of this stage.

### Version 2: the pond

The magazine site worked, but it was basic, looked very AI-generated, and was not delightful. For the redesign I had the idea of using the metaphor of a "pond" on the homepage, with different interactive elements and a "river" to show my career progression. I also added the "Ask about me" chat backed by my own API.

From there I iterated in the lab. The flat river became a painted pond, the fonts changed to Cormorant and Karla, and a color scale taken from the painting replaced the old palette across every page. I rejected plenty along the way: foggy overlays, hyper-realistic lily pads, a sidebar that jumped around.

## Learnings and pivots

**Compare before committing.** The lab changed how I made decisions. Seeing five versions side by side made it easier for me to pinpoint aspects I liked and what I wanted to implement on the actual site.

**Clean surface, deep docs.** I stopped trying to make the whole site look unconventional. The landing page carries the personality; the inner pages lean on Mintlify's familiar structure so content is easy to find.

**Reverting is part of the process.** Several changes looked good in a prompt and wrong on the page. Keeping the original as the baseline and changing one thing at a time made it easy to go back.

**Writing in my own voice.** It's easy to rely on AI to generate content, but I wrote the case studies and articles myself, including the "Idea and reasoning" section on this page. Part of being a good designer and writer now is having a strong vision and knowing when to step in with your own voice when AI tools don't get it right.
